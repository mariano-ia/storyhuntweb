The Islands NYC Forgot

The Staten Island Ferry has carried passengers since 1905, made free to all riders in 1997, and now moves roughly 70,000 people a day between the St. George Terminal in Staten Island and Whitehall Terminal in lower Manhattan. Most ride it for the five-mile view of the Statue of Liberty and the Financial District skyline — which is legitimately one of the best free spectacles in New York. But the ferry passes within a quarter mile of something most passengers never look for: Ellis Island's south side, where the abandoned hospital complex sits in a state of deliberate ruin. The main immigration hall had its last visitors before it was restored; the contagious disease wards were closed in 1954 and have not been touched since. Their windows are still broken. Their medical equipment is still inside. A coalition called Save Ellis Island runs occasional hard-hat tours, but the south side remains one of the few places in New York City where the 20th century has simply been left where it fell.

North Brother Island, visible from the Bronx just north of Rikers Island in the East River, is now a bird sanctuary closed to the public — which means almost no one alive has stood on it. In 1904 the General Slocum excursion steamship caught fire nearby, killing 1,021 people in what remains the deadliest disaster in New York City history until September 11. North Brother Island was where survivors were brought ashore. It later became the site of Riverside Hospital, where the city quarantined tuberculosis patients from 1885 to 1963 — including Mary Mallon, the Irish cook known as Typhoid Mary, who spent her last 23 years confined there in a cottage called Bungalow 2. The island was abandoned in 1963 and the forest grew back fast: cormorants now nest in the old hospital's second-floor wards. Then there's U Thant Island — also called Belmont Island — a 0.4-acre strip of granite in the East River between Midtown Manhattan and Queens, topped with a Buddhist peace stupa placed there in 1977 by a Sri Lankan diplomat. It is technically accessible only with a permit, technically owned by the city, and technically the smallest island in the five boroughs.

A StoryHunt along NYC's waterfront traces these hidden geographies using the ferry network as your transit system. The hunt begins at Pier 17 in the Seaport District — where clipper ships were loaded with goods for China in the 1840s, and where the cobblestones still show the iron rings used to tie off their lines — and uses the NYC Ferry routes launched in May 2017 to move you across the water. You'll decode the signal flags used by harbor pilots, find the tidal gauge that the Army Corps of Engineers has maintained since 1920, and learn which buildings on the Brooklyn waterfront were built specifically to face the water because that's where the money came from. The best city views aren't from the top. They're from the water, at eye level, where everything looks exactly as precarious as it actually is.
