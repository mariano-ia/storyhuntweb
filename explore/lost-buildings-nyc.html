The Buildings NYC Chose to Destroy

Stand at the corner of Seventh Avenue and 33rd Street today and you're standing inside a ghost. The original Pennsylvania Station — designed by McKim, Mead & White and completed in 1910 — occupied every inch of the two blocks you now see around Madison Square Garden. Its waiting room was modeled on the Baths of Caracalla in Rome: 150 feet high, flooded with natural light through iron-and-glass vaults, clad in pink Milford granite quarried in Massachusetts. More than 650,000 square feet of one of the greatest public rooms ever built in America. When the Pennsylvania Railroad sold it to developers in 1963, demolition crews took three years to bring it down. Architecture critic Ada Louise Huxtable wrote at the time: "We will probably be judged not by the monuments we build but by those we have destroyed." She was right. Penn Station's loss directly triggered the creation of New York City's Landmarks Preservation Commission in 1965 — every landmark that has survived since owes its survival, in part, to that act of civic vandalism.

A few blocks north and east, at the corner of Fifth Avenue and 33rd Street, stood the original Waldorf-Astoria — actually two hotels built side by side by feuding cousins. William Waldorf Astor opened the Waldorf in 1893; his cousin John Jacob Astor IV responded by tearing down his own adjacent mansion and opening the Astoria four years later. The hyphen connecting their two names was both architectural and symbolic — a door that could be locked from either side at any time. For a generation, the Waldorf-Astoria was the social center of New York, the place where high society gathered and deals were struck over consommé. In 1929, the cousins' heirs sold both buildings, the Empire State Building Corporation demolished the entire complex, and by 1931 the Empire State Building rose in its place. The new Waldorf-Astoria opened the same year on Park Avenue, where it still stands — but it was never quite the same address.
