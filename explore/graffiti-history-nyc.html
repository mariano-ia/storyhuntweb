The City as Canvas

It began with a teenager from Washington Heights. Around 1970, Demetrius — known as Taki 183, the "183" being his home address on 183rd Street — started writing his name on walls, lamp posts, and subway cars across all five boroughs. A New York Times profile in 1971 made him famous and inadvertently launched a movement. Within two years, hundreds of kids across the city were doing the same thing, each developing a personal signature. By 1973, writers like Tracy 168 in the Bronx and Phase 2 in Harlem were painting entire subway cars in multicolor "wildstyle" lettering so complex it was nearly unreadable — which was partly the point. The MTA ran what it called a "buff" operation from the beginning, covering pieces with grey paint within hours of their appearance. The writers responded by working faster and more elaborately. The arms race between the city and its young artists defined New York for two decades.

The 1980s brought the gallery world downtown, and with it a strange transformation. Dondi White, who painted whole subway cars in the late 1970s with works like "Children of the Grave," was showing in galleries in SoHo. Lee Quiñones painted handball courts on Delancey Street as large-scale murals; his work at the corner of Stanton and Forsyth still draws people who know what they're looking at. Lady Pink — Sandra Fabara — was one of the few women in the early scene, painting trains from 1979 and later becoming one of the first writers to cross from the subway into fine art. Keith Haring, who was not a subway writer in the traditional sense, filled blank advertising panels in the 125th Street station with chalk drawings between 1980 and 1985 — a practice that was technically illegal and deeply beloved. The MTA's "Clean Train Movement" succeeded in 1989 in removing all graffiti from the active fleet, effectively ending the subway era, but by then the culture had already escaped its original container.

The story didn't end — it relocated. 5 Pointz in Long Island City was a warehouse complex that became the world's most concentrated outdoor museum of graffiti from the 1990s until 2013, when its owner whitewashed it overnight and converted it to luxury condos. A court eventually awarded $6.75 million to 49 artists whose work was destroyed without notice. Banksy's 2013 month-long residency, "Better Out Than In," brought the British artist to the city daily, leaving new pieces across all five boroughs and turning the hunt for his work into a real-time scavenger game. A StoryHunt through NYC's graffiti history connects those threads: it leads you to surviving walls, explains the legal battles and the aesthetics, and asks you to decode the messages hidden in plain sight across a city that has been writing to itself for over fifty years.
