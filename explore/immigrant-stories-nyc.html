The Neighborhoods That Built New York

At the turn of the twentieth century, the Lower East Side was the most densely populated place on Earth — over 700 people per acre in some blocks, most of them Jewish immigrants from Russia, Poland, and the Austro-Hungarian Empire. The building at 97 Orchard Street, now the Tenement Museum, housed nearly 7,000 residents across 97 years before it was sealed in 1935. The apartment of the Gumpertz family, garment workers from Prussia, still looks the way it did when Nathalie Gumpertz's husband vanished in 1874, leaving her to raise four children by taking in piecework. A few blocks east, the Essex Street Market — first opened by Mayor Fiorello La Guardia in 1940 to give pushcart vendors a permanent indoor home — still operates, though now in a new building on Delancey Street. The pushcarts themselves are gone, but the logic hasn't changed: immigrants selling food to immigrants, generation after generation.

Little Italy once stretched from Canal Street to Houston along Mulberry, Mott, and Elizabeth — a neighborhood of over 10,000 Southern Italian immigrants who began arriving in the 1880s. The Feast of San Gennaro has been held on Mulberry Street every September since 1926, a tribute to the patron saint of Naples that outlasted nearly every Italian family in the neighborhood. By the 1990s, Chinatown had expanded north and west until Little Italy was compressed into a few tourist-facing blocks. Walk down Mott Street today and you cross an invisible border: south of Canal, the signs are in Mandarin and the voices are in Cantonese; north of it, the restaurants are Italian-named but the produce vendors speak Fujianese. The first Chinese residents settled near 8 Mott Street in the 1870s, forming what would become one of the largest Chinese communities in the Western hemisphere.

For a different kind of immigrant geography, the 7 train into Queens is a revelation. Jackson Heights — centered around Roosevelt Avenue and 74th Street — has been called the most ethnically diverse urban neighborhood on Earth, and the title is not hyperbole. Gujarati jewelers, Bangladeshi sweet shops, Colombian bakeries, and Nepali restaurants occupy the same block, sometimes the same building. The neighborhood's history as an immigrant gateway accelerated after the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended the quota system that had favored Northern Europeans. Brighton Beach in Brooklyn tells a different story: "Little Odessa" became home to Russian and Ukrainian Jewish immigrants who arrived in waves through the 1970s and 1980s, filling the boardwalk with Russian-language bookshops, delis stocked with smoked fish and black bread, and restaurants where the vodka arrives before the menus. A StoryHunt through these neighborhoods sends you beneath the surface of the visible city, into the history that most people walk through without ever reading.
