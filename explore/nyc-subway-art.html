The Gallery Beneath the City

The New York City subway contains more than 300 permanent artworks installed across its 472 stations — the result of the MTA's Arts & Design program, which since 1985 has commissioned artists to create site-specific works in tile, glass, steel, and bronze throughout the system. Most riders walk past them without a second glance. At the 81st Street station on the B and C lines, beneath the American Museum of Natural History on Central Park West, sculptor Tom Otterness installed 140 bronze figures throughout the station in 2001. Tiny bronze men with round heads carry briefcases and hammer coins; a large bronze alligator devours a man in a top hat; a woman tumbles through a giant coin slot. The entire composition is a surreal underground world most passengers don't notice until their third or fourth time through the station.

The original 1904 IRT stations — the first in the system — were designed by the architecture firm Heins & LaFarge, who used ornate Guastavino tile vaults and hand-laid ceramic mosaics to identify each stop. The City Hall station, which closed to regular passengers in 1945 and remains sealed beneath the Brooklyn Bridge–City Hall station today, has its original Tiffany glass skylights and Roman brick vaulting still intact. It is one of the most beautiful rooms in New York City, and almost no one has ever seen it inside. The New York Transit Museum occasionally offers tours, but demand vastly exceeds supply. At the 72nd Street station on the 1/2/3 lines, the walls still carry the original green and white terra cotta tiles from 1904, designed to help illiterate immigrants identify their stop by color and pattern. Every decoration was functional first and beautiful second — a constraint that produced something extraordinary.

More recent commissions have pushed the form further. At the 34th Street–Hudson Yards station, which opened in 2015 as the westernmost stop on the 7 line, artist Xenobia Bailey installed a 300-foot ceiling mosaic called Funktional Vibrations, a swirling pattern of crocheted-style mandalas in vivid orange, purple, and gold that stretches the entire length of the mezzanine. At the Fulton Center in Lower Manhattan, artist James Carpenter created a giant oculus of reflective panels that bounces natural daylight down from the street into the underground concourse. These aren't decorations added to a transit system — they are arguments that public infrastructure can be art, and that the city has an obligation to make the daily experience of commuting beautiful. StoryHunt uses the subway's hidden artworks as a series of clues, sending you through stations with new eyes, looking for details that turn a commute into a puzzle and a platform into a gallery.
