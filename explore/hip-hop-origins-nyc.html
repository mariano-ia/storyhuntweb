Where Hip Hop Was Born: The South Bronx Story

On August 11, 1973, eighteen-year-old Clive Campbell — known as DJ Kool Herc — threw a back-to-school party in the recreation room of 1520 Sedgwick Avenue in the South Bronx. The address is now a New York City landmark. Herc had two turntables and a technique he called the Merry-Go-Round: he isolated the drum break sections of funk and soul records and looped them continuously, switching between two copies of the same record to extend the instrumental breakdown indefinitely. Dancers went wild during those breaks. Herc called them B-boys and B-girls. What happened in that basement recreation room didn't stay there.

The South Bronx of 1973 was in crisis. Robert Moses had driven the Cross Bronx Expressway — a six-lane highway — straight through the heart of the neighborhood between 1948 and 1963, displacing 60,000 residents and destroying a functioning middle-class community. Landlords, unable to sell or rent their buildings in the hollowed-out aftermath, hired arsonists for insurance money. Between 1970 and 1980, the South Bronx lost 40 percent of its housing stock to fire. Hip hop emerged from that rubble. Afrika Bambaataa, another Bronx DJ, built on Herc's foundation from his base at 1595 White Plains Road, founding the Universal Zulu Nation in 1973 to redirect gang energy into music, dance, and graffiti. Grandmaster Flash refined the cutting technique, developed the "clock theory" of beat timing, and with Melle Mel wrote "The Message" in 1982 — a track that described life on Bronx streets so precisely that radio stations initially refused to play it. The New York Times called it "the most important record of the year." It had been recorded at Sugar Hill Studios in Englewood, New Jersey, but every bar of it was soaked in the Bronx.
