Fashion Avenue's Factory Floor

Walk down 7th Avenue between 34th and 42nd Streets and the signage says "Fashion Avenue" — a rebranding from 1972 meant to attract the kind of glossy attention the district had long stopped receiving. But step into the side streets and the original industry hasn't entirely vanished. On West 38th Street, narrow storefronts still sell spools of thread in 500 colors, industrial zippers by the gross, and sequined trim by the yard. The stretch between 6th and 9th Avenues that once employed over 300,000 workers — most of them Jewish and Italian immigrants living in nearby tenements — still has fabric wholesalers and pattern-making studios tucked above nail salons and tourist shops. The giant thimble-and-button sculpture at 7th Avenue and 39th Street marks the spot like a tombstone for an industry that made New York the fashion capital of the world.

During World War II, the Garment District pivoted almost overnight. Hundreds of factories that had been turning out women's blouses and men's suits retooled to produce military uniforms, parachutes, and field jackets for the U.S. Army. At the peak of wartime production, Midtown's loft buildings ran three shifts. The International Ladies' Garment Workers' Union — founded in 1900, headquartered at 275 7th Avenue — had negotiated the terms that made those workers' lives survivable: overtime pay, a minimum wage, a 35-hour week. Those victories came after the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire of March 1911, which killed 146 garment workers just a few blocks south of the district's center. The building at 23-29 Washington Place, where the fire broke out on the top three floors, still stands in Greenwich Village. The locked exit doors that trapped the workers led directly to the labor protections that the ILGWU would spend decades enforcing.

Today, Mood Fabrics at 225 West 37th Street — made famous by the TV show Project Runway — draws designers from around the world to its four floors of fabric. M&J Trimming at 1008 Sixth Avenue has sold buttons, ribbons, and rhinestones since 1936. These are not nostalgia shops; they are working suppliers to the small-batch studios and independent designers who have quietly returned to the district in recent years. A StoryHunt through the Garment District traces the full arc: from the sweatshops of the 1880s to the union halls of the 1940s to the showrooms of today, with clues hidden in loading docks, carved into building facades, and printed on the backs of forgotten trade cards. This is the neighborhood where every thread has a story.
