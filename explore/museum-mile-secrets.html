Museum Mile: Beyond the Exhibits

The Metropolitan Museum of Art at 1000 Fifth Avenue holds more than two million objects in its collection. On any given day, visitors see roughly 5,000 of them. The rest sit in climate-controlled storage vaults spread across five floors beneath the building — Roman bronzes in crates, Egyptian canopic jars stacked on steel shelving, thousands of paintings that haven't seen daylight in decades. The one object no one misses is also the most impossible to hide: the Temple of Dendur, a complete ancient Egyptian temple built around 15 BC, now reassembled in a glass-enclosed hall facing Central Park. Egypt gave it to the United States in 1965 as thanks for American help funding the UNESCO rescue of monuments threatened by the Aswan High Dam. It traveled across the Atlantic in 661 crates. The Met was chosen over the Smithsonian and eight other competing institutions.

Two blocks north at 1071 Fifth Avenue, Frank Lloyd Wright's Guggenheim Museum was one of the most controversial buildings in New York history before it ever opened. The city rejected the building permit application sixteen times between 1943 and 1956 due to objections about the unconventional structure and local zoning codes. Twenty-one artists — including Willem de Kooning and Robert Motherwell — signed an open letter opposing the spiral design, arguing that no flat canvas could be properly displayed on a curved, sloping wall. Wright died six months before the building opened in 1959, never seeing it finished. The museum's ramp descends from the top floor to the bottom, not bottom to top as most visitors assume; Wright intended viewers to take the elevator up and walk down through the collection, following gravity rather than fighting it. The top of the spiral, rarely opened to the public, contains a small rotunda with a glass oculus that was Wright's original preferred entry point to the entire building.

At 1048 Fifth Avenue, the Neue Galerie occupies the former mansion of Benjamin and Caroline Astor, later bought by grain magnate William Starr Miller. Its centerpiece is Gustav Klimt's 1907 masterpiece "Portrait of Adele Bloch-Bauer I" — better known as "Woman in Gold" — which the Nazi government seized from the Bloch-Bauer family in 1938 after annexing Austria. The painting spent sixty years in the Österreichische Galerie Belvedere in Vienna, exhibited as Austrian national heritage, before Maria Altmann, the niece of Adele Bloch-Bauer, sued the Austrian government for its return. The Austrian Arbitration Panel ruled in her favor in 2006. The Neue Galerie purchased the painting for $135 million, then the highest price ever paid for a work of art. A StoryHunt through Museum Mile traces these parallel dramas — the theft, the legal battles, the architectural fights, and the vast invisible collection that lives beneath Fifth Avenue — turning one of the world's most visited streets into an unsolved mystery.
