The Literary Underground of New York City

From the 1890s through the 1960s, the stretch of Fourth Avenue between Astor Place and Union Square was known as "Book Row" — a corridor of over forty used bookshops where you could spend an afternoon lost in first editions, pulp fiction, and forgotten philosophy. Almost all of them are gone now. The Strand Bookstore, founded by Ben Bass in 1927 at 81 Fourth Avenue before moving to its current home at 828 Broadway, is the last survivor. Today it houses eighteen miles of books across four floors, including a rare book room on the third floor where signed first editions sit behind locked glass. The store has outlasted every competitor on the street and every economic crisis the city has thrown at it, including the postwar collapse of the secondhand book trade and a battle against corporate retail that lasted decades.

But the Strand is only the most visible part of New York City's bookstore underground. For years, a man named Michael Seidenberg ran Brazenhead Books — an appointment-only shop inside his own apartment on East 84th Street on the Upper East Side. You had to know someone who knew someone to get in. The apartment was floor-to-ceiling books, with Seidenberg serving wine to guests who stayed until midnight debating what they'd found. He died in 2018, and the shop died with him, but it inspired a generation of underground readers who had experienced what a bookstore could be when it was also a living room. On the other end of the spectrum, Housing Works Bookshop at 126 Crosby Street in SoHo has operated since 1999 with every dollar of profit going toward housing and services for New Yorkers living with HIV and AIDS — a radical use of the secondhand book trade that would have been recognizable to the dealers of old Book Row.

A StoryHunt through NYC's literary world sends you down these rabbit holes yourself. You'll decode clues rooted in real publication history — the neighborhoods where certain writers lived, the now-gone cafés where manuscripts were revised, the buildings that still carry faint evidence of the print shops, lending libraries, and radical presses that once occupied their ground floors. Three Lives & Company in the West Village has been a curated independent since 1978 at 154 West 10th Street; the Argosy Book Store on East 59th Street has been in the same family since 1925. These places survived because someone cared enough to fight for them. Your hunt through their history is a way of understanding why.
