The Secret History of NYC's Waterfront

Manhattan was built on water. The Dutch colony of New Amsterdam planted itself at the southern tip of the island in 1626 specifically because of its natural deepwater harbor, and for the next three centuries, the waterfront was where the real money moved. By the mid-1800s, South Street — the stretch of piers between Fulton Street and Pike Slip — was the busiest port on the planet. The counting houses along Front Street held more capital than most European banks. The sailors who worked Pier 17 unloaded silk, sugar, and contraband in equal measure, and the narrow alleys behind the warehouses were well-known corridors for goods that never appeared in any manifest. The Seamen's Church Institute at 25 South Street kept a log of men who disappeared from those docks and were never seen again.

The West Side piers — from Gansevoort Street up to the 50s — became the domain of the International Longshoremen's Association in the early 20th century, and the ILA ran them like a private empire. The shape-up system, where dockers gathered at dawn and foremen picked men by hand, was a mechanism of control and tribute as much as labor allocation. Pier 54, at West 14th Street, is where the RMS Carpathia docked on April 18, 1912, carrying 705 Titanic survivors; a crowd of 40,000 stood in the rain on the West Side Highway to meet them. The same pier watched the Lusitania sail for Liverpool in May 1915, never to return. Both piers were demolished. The iron arch that marked Pier 54's entrance now stands alone on a concrete slab in Hudson River Park — the only remnant of the entire West Side pier complex. In 1962 a fire ripped through the pier sheds from 44th to 52nd Streets and left nothing but pilings.

What replaced the working piers tells a stranger story than the piers themselves. The High Line was built in 1934 as an elevated freight railroad running through the interior of meatpacking and warehouse buildings along the Far West Side — the city had lost so many people to grade-crossing accidents on West Side Avenue that it was nicknamed "Death Avenue." The elevated line stopped running in 1980; a single train of frozen turkeys was its last cargo. For twenty years, the structure slowly became a linear wilderness of wildflowers and weeds, forty feet above street level, and urban explorers slipped through fences to walk it in secret. When the city finally turned it into a park in 2009, the designers kept patches of the spontaneous vegetation that had reclaimed the tracks. A StoryHunt through NYC's waterfront traces all of this — the Titanic pier, the smugglers' alleys, the Death Avenue crossings — through a neighborhood that rewrites itself every generation but never quite escapes its own past.
