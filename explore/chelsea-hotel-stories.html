The Chelsea Hotel: Every Room Has a Body Count

The Chelsea Hotel opened at 222 West 23rd Street in 1884, originally built as one of New York City's first cooperative apartment buildings. Its red-brick facade, ornate iron balconies, and twelve stories made it the tallest building in Manhattan for a brief period. By 1905 it had converted to a hotel, and for the next century it operated as something the city has never quite replicated: a residential hotel for artists, where long-term tenants paid low rent in exchange for the building accepting their work as partial payment. Mark Twain and O. Henry lived there in the early years. Thomas Wolfe wrote "You Can't Go Home Again" in a suite on an upper floor. Dylan Thomas, the Welsh poet, was drinking at the White Horse Tavern on Hudson Street in November 1953 when he collapsed; he was staying at the Chelsea and returned there before being taken to St. Vincent's Hospital, where he died four days later. His room became the first of many that the building wore like battle scars.

Arthur C. Clarke wrote "2001: A Space Odyssey" in room 1008 in 1964, reportedly describing the experience as writing in "the world's most famous and least pretentious hotel." Edie Sedgwick, Andy Warhol's downtown muse, lived there in the mid-1960s while her world was collapsing around her. Leonard Cohen had a brief relationship with Janis Joplin — they met in the elevator — and later wrote "Chelsea Hotel No. 2" about it, one of the most specific and unsentimental love songs in the canon. Then came October 12, 1978: Sid Vicious of the Sex Pistols was staying in room 100 with Nancy Spungen when she was found stabbed to death on the bathroom floor. Sid was charged with murder, released on bail, and died of a heroin overdose at a party in Greenwich Village in February 1979, before the case ever went to trial. The Chelsea eventually changed the room numbers to deter morbid tourists. It didn't stop anyone.

A StoryHunt through Chelsea puts you on the pavement outside the hotel's iron front and works outward from there — through the gallery district that grew up around it on West 22nd and 24th Streets, past the diner where hotel residents ate for decades, and into the quieter blocks where the artists who couldn't afford the Chelsea rented cold-water flats. Every clue connects to a real name and a real room number. You don't need a guide to understand what happened here. You need curiosity, a willingness to stand still on a sidewalk and imagine it, and a phone with StoryHunt loaded.
