Crypts, Chapels, and Sacred Spaces Hidden Across NYC

St. Paul's Chapel at 209 Broadway is the oldest public building in continuous use in Manhattan, completed in 1766 and still holding services today. It survived the Great Fire of 1776 when much of lower Manhattan burned, and George Washington worshipped here after his first inauguration in 1789 — his pew, boxed in dark wood, still sits in the north aisle exactly where he sat. Directly behind the chapel, through the iron cemetery fence, are graves dating to the colonial era, headstones leaning at odd angles with names worn smooth by 250 winters. After September 11, 2001, St. Paul's became a relief center for rescue workers at Ground Zero; the volunteers draped handmade quilts over the old pews, and the marks they left on the walls were preserved exactly as they were. You can walk inside for free on any weekday.

St. Patrick's Old Cathedral on Mulberry Street — the original St. Patrick's, completed in 1815, not the famous Fifth Avenue one that opened in 1879 — contains a system of underground vaults and catacombs that stretch beneath the cemetery on its grounds. These vaults hold the remains of some of the most powerful figures in early New York Catholic history, including the first Archbishop of New York, John Hughes, who commissioned the Fifth Avenue cathedral and died before it was completed. The old cathedral's exterior wall, a solid brick barrier running the length of Mulberry Street, was built to withstand anti-Catholic riots that were a real threat in 1830s New York. Today the neighborhood around it is gentrified NoLIta, but the wall still stands, still enclosing the same ground.

The Church of the Transfiguration at 1 East 29th Street — known to New Yorkers as the Little Church Around the Corner — earned its nickname in 1870 when a nearby church refused to hold the funeral of an actor named George Holland, calling actors disreputable people. The rector of the Transfiguration agreed to perform the service. From that day forward, the theatrical community claimed it as their own, and it became the church where actors married, baptized their children, and were buried. Stained glass windows dedicated to Edwin Booth, the greatest American actor of the 19th century, still illuminate the nave, alongside memorials to dozens of performers whose names filled Broadway marquees a century ago. A StoryHunt through NYC's hidden sacred spaces connects you to these specific human stories: not just the architecture, but the acts of grace and defiance that gave each building its meaning.
